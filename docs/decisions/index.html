Other choices we made

Beyond the main question, five smaller choices shaped the plan. Each one follows the same pattern: the question, the options, what we picked, and why.

  1. 1

    What should the framework hold about people?

    • Client records or a registry of people with AFN
    • ✓ Counts per area (census tract, ZIP, island), with small counts hidden by the owner
    • Nothing about people; hazards and shelters only

    We chose: Counts per area, with small counts hidden by the owner

    The brief rules out personal data and registries; a planner needs how many and where, not who. Counts per area are what the public sources already give, and what a partner can share without a records agreement once small counts are hidden. Leaving people out would miss the point of the challenge.

    “Out of scope: use of personally identifiable client data; individual-level registries or tracking”

    DHS challenge brief, p. 6
    Show the evidence (2)
    • HHS emPOWER publishes electricity-dependent Medicare beneficiaries per ZIP and shows 1 to 10 as 11; the State has 7,133. source, checked 2026-11-14
    • The project's privacy levels (public, counts only, restricted) are matched to the State's four tiers in CDO-004. source, checked 2026-11-14
  2. 2

    Invent our own field names, or reuse existing standards?

    • Invent a new ESF-6 data standard
    • ✓ Reuse field names from DCAT-US, HSDS, FEMA NSS and NIMS, adding only what none of them has

    We chose: Reuse, and add only four things: area level, last confirmed date, privacy level, ladder level

    Every label field maps to a field the State portals, FEMA or 211-style directories already use, so a label can be published by the State portal or read by FEMA's system with no new software. A new standard would be one more thing for partners to learn.

    “Security: solutions must align with State of Hawaiʻi data governance and security policies.”

    DHS challenge brief, p. 5
    Show the evidence (1)
    • Label fields map to DCAT-US 3.0 (dataset), HSDS 3.x `service_capacity` and `assured_date` (partner pledges), and NSS shelter fields such as `generator_onsite` and `wheelchair_accessible`; only `area_level`, `confirmed_on`, `privacy` and `level` are new. source, checked 2026-11-14
  3. 3

    A live web app, or a brief that prints?

    • A live web app that needs a connection and a login
    • ✓ A static site with no login, no server of its own and no outside services (it runs from a laptop with Wi-Fi off), plus a printable brief (US Letter, black and white) per county and hazard

    We chose: Static site and printable briefs

    Planning happens in offices, but response happens where Wi-Fi and power fail. A page that calls no outside service, and a brief that works on paper, keep working when the connection does not. It also needs no server for anyone to maintain.

    “Usability: DHS staff and partners must be able to realistically use the framework for planning and during high-stress, low-connectivity conditions.”

    DHS challenge brief, p. 5
    Show the evidence (1)
    • Honolulu's July 2025 tsunami after-action report asks for "a list of open assembly areas in a printable format"; the City's live shelter pages need JavaScript and show only what is open now. source, checked 2026-11-14
  4. 4

    Where should AI be used?

    • Let an AI model produce the estimates directly
    • ✓ Compute every number with plain, repeatable code from labeled sources; use AI agents to build the pipeline and pages, research and cross-check sources, and draft text

    We chose: Numbers from plain code; AI to build, research, check and draft

    Estimates that counties must validate have to come out the same every time and be traceable to a source and a stated assumption. AI did the labor that usually makes this too slow (finding, saving and re-checking hundreds of sources; writing the code; drafting), and a second agent re-opened every citation.

    “County, DHS, and partner organizations to validate as reasonable [target: e.g., within an agreed margin against past events].”

    DHS challenge brief, p. 7
    Show the evidence (2)
    • Every quote in the planning factors was checked against the downloaded file's text by a script; shelter-seeking rates for tsunami and flood are marked team_assumption because no published rate was found.
    • Past Oʻahu hurricane turnouts with the same whole-island exposure ranged from 34 (Olivia 2018) to 1,114 (Lane 2018), so the back-test shows the model's low-to-high range next to every published count and does not tune rates to the events.
  5. 5

    Which hazards to cover?

    • ✓ Four hazards: hurricane, wildfire, tsunami, flood
    • Five, adding earthquake

    We chose: Four hazards, with earthquake named as a later addition on the same ladder

    The brief's objectives, scope and first measurable goal all name the same four hazards. Earthquake appears once in the brief, in the list of planning challenges (and in the pitch). Our research did not look for an earthquake exposure layer, so we leave it out rather than guess; it would be one more hazard on the same labels and ladder.

    “Produce hazard-specific AFN vulnerability maps for all four hazard types covering every county [target: 100% of census tracts].”

    DHS challenge brief, p. 7
    Show the evidence (1)
    • Objectives: "Map where AFN populations live and how exposed they are to hurricanes, wildfires, tsunamis, and floods."
