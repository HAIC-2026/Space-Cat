Step 1 of 6

What Hawaiʻi asked for

Hawaiʻi's Department of Human Services (DHS) set a challenge for the 2026 Hawaiʻi Annual Innovation Challenge: help disaster planners know, before a disaster, who will need extra help, where they live, and what help is ready for them.

Who this is for

A kūpuna (an elder) on home oxygen, living alone. A wildfire starts. Her oxygen needs power, and she has no car. Is she already in someone's plan?

The problem, in DHS's words

“The information exists. It just isn’t connected.”

DHS pitch deck, slide 5

“A fragmented information landscape leads to fragmented preparation, uneven or just-in-time responses, and slower recovery.”

DHS challenge brief, p. 2

The facts about who needs help sit with many different owners: State and county agencies, nonprofits, utilities, health plans. Each keeps its own list, in its own words. Planners rebuild the picture by hand, by phone, for every storm.

Three terms you'll see

Mass care (ESF-6)
The part of an emergency plan that covers shelter, food, water and basic care for people forced from home. Its official name is Emergency Support Function 6. Each county runs it; the State supports them.
Access and functional needs (AFN)
People who need extra help in a disaster. Planners sort these needs into five areas, called CMIST:
  • CCommunication

    limited English, hearing or vision loss, cognitive challenges

  • MMedical

    power-dependent equipment like home oxygen, medications, dialysis, home care

  • IIndependence

    trouble walking, help with daily living, wheelchairs and other devices

  • SSafety and support

    relies on a caregiver, behavioral health needs, minor children

  • TTransportation

    no car, relies on paratransit or family for rides

Census tract
A neighborhood-sized area the U.S. Census uses, usually 2,000 to 6,000 people. Hawaiʻi has 432 tracts where people live.

What DHS will measure

For four hazards: hurricane, wildfire, tsunami and flood.

  1. 1

    Map who needs help, for every neighborhood

    For all four hazards, covering every census tract in the state.

    DHS's exact words

    “Produce hazard-specific AFN vulnerability maps for all four hazard types covering every county [target: 100% of census tracts].” DHS challenge brief, p. 7

  2. 2

    Estimate what they will need

    Shelter, food, power, transport and more, checked against past disasters.

    DHS's exact words

    “Generate needs estimates for each hazard across the CMIST categories. County, DHS, and partner organizations to validate as reasonable [target: e.g., within an agreed margin against past events].” DHS challenge brief, p. 7

  3. 3

    Make planning faster

    Cut the time it takes to put together a coordinated plan for an event.

    DHS's exact words

    “Reduce the time to produce a coordinated needs-and-resources plan for an event from [current time] to [target time]. Teams may propose their own baseline and target time, with justification.” DHS challenge brief, p. 7
