Step 4 of 6

What we chose, and why

Our answer: standardize the label, not the system. Leave every source where it is. Describe each one the same way, so anyone can tell what it says, how old it is and who to ask. Then help each owner share it a little better, one step at a time.

Part 1

The label

A one-page description that goes on every data source, the same way on all of them:

  • who owns it and how to reach them,
  • what it covers, and for what size of area,
  • when someone last confirmed it was true,
  • how private it is,
  • and its limits: what it can't tell you.

It reuses fields from standards the State already publishes (DCAT-US) and adds only four: area level, last confirmed, privacy level and ladder level. Nothing new to buy or install.

Part 2

The ladder

Four levels for how a source is shared today. Each owner climbs at its own pace, and every step is useful even if the next one never comes. Nobody waits on a statewide agreement.

  1. 0

    Phone call

    Ask the owner and wait.

    A planner can: See on the brief that the fact exists, who holds it (office or role) and exactly what to ask, instead of starting from a blank page.

    Example: Utility medical-needs customer counts (Hawaiian Electric, KIUC)

    11 of our 57 sources are here today

  2. 1

    Spreadsheet on a schedule

    Published on a schedule, readable without asking.

    A planner can: Use the number with its date, and see when it is past its refresh date. The call happens once a season instead of once an event.

    Example: DHS Databook (yearly PDF with program counts by island)

    19 of our 57 sources are here today

  3. 2

    File at a stable address

    A program can fetch it and join it with other sources by place.

    A planner can: Get the current file without asking anyone; sources line up by place code without retyping.

    Example: FEMA ESF#6 Shelter System (stable address and shared fields, but no label and many blank fields)

    16 of our 57 sources are here today

  4. 3

    Service that answers questions

    Answers “how many, here, today” with counts, never records.

    A planner can: Get a current count during planning or an event, with its label, without waiting for a file.

    Example: HHS emPOWER (counts per ZIP, 1 to 10 shown as 11)

    11 of our 57 sources are here today

Why this option

  • It works with what exists. Owners keep their data where it is. Nothing new to buy or install.
  • Every step pays off on its own. Nothing waits on a statewide agreement.
  • It's honest about gaps. A missing fact shows up as a phone call, with who to call and what to ask.
  • It has worked elsewhere. Transit agencies grew a shared format step by step, and HHS emPOWER already shares health counts without sharing records.
Show the evidence (3)
  • The State already asks for labels: CDO-003 Data Catalog Guidelines (28 August 2025) say each agency should keep a catalog and metadata for its data assets and name a data steward for each. Both State portals already publish DCAT-US records with owner, contact, address and last-changed date on every dataset. source, checked 2026-11-14
  • Ladders and thin standards have worked elsewhere: the 5-star open data scheme lets a publisher stop at any step; GTFS "started with a collaboration between TriMet in Portland, Oregon, and Google" and now reports "over 10,000 agencies in 100+ countries". source, checked 2026-11-14
  • A level-3 source for people already exists and protects privacy: HHS emPOWER answers with counts per ZIP and masks 1 to 10 as 11. source, checked 2026-11-14

What this looks like for a planner: one printable brief per county and hazard. Every number names its label. Every gap says who to ask. The next step shows it working on real public data.
