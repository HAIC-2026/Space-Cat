Step 4 of 6

What we chose, and why

Our answer: standardize the label, not the system. Leave every source where it is. Describe each one the same way, so anyone can tell what it says, how old it is and who to ask. Then help each owner share it a little better, one step at a time.

Part 1

The label

A one-page description that goes on every data source, the same way on all of them:

  • who owns it and how to reach them,
  • what it covers, and for what size of area,
  • when someone last confirmed it was true,
  • how private it is,
  • and its limits: what it can't tell you.

It reuses fields from standards the State already publishes (DCAT-US) and adds only four: area level, last confirmed, privacy level and ladder level. Nothing new to buy or install.

Part 2

The ladder

Four levels for how a source is shared today. Level 3 is the top of the ladder and the goal every organization works toward over time. Getting there is a gradual transition, not a single switch: each owner climbs at its own pace, and every step is useful even if the next one takes years. Nobody waits on a statewide agreement.

Where sources startGoal: Level 3
  1. 0

    Phone call

    The information exists, but the only way to get it is to ask the owner and wait.

    An AI agent planner can: Name the organization to call and the question to ask, and draft the request. A person has to make the request; the agent cannot get the information itself.

    Example: Hawaiian Electric and KIUC know how many customers rely on power for medical equipment, but they do not publish it. A planner has to call.

    11 of our 57 sources are here today

  2. 1

    Spreadsheet on a schedule

    The owner publishes it, or sends it on a known schedule, in a form a person can read without asking anyone.

    An AI agent planner can: Read the latest file without calling, quote the number with its 'as of' and 'last confirmed' dates, and warn when it is past its refresh date. The owner answers once a season instead of during every event.

    Example: DHS Databook: a yearly PDF with program counts by island. A person can read it, but a program cannot pull the numbers out.

    19 of our 57 sources are here today

  3. 2

    File at a stable address

    A program can fetch it and join it to other sources by place, without a person reformatting it.

    An AI agent planner can: Fetch the latest file at any time, join it to other sources by place code, and add it up by area without a person reformatting it.

    Example: Census disability counts per census tract: a file at a fixed Census address that a program can download and line up with hazard maps.

    16 of our 57 sources are here today

  4. 3

    Highest level · the goal

    Service that answers questions

    A live service answers questions like 'how many, in this area, as of today' and returns only the answer, so an AI agent can ask it directly. About people, it gives counts only, never records.

    An AI agent planner can: Ask the service directly during planning or an event (for example through a REST query or a Model Context Protocol tool) and get a current count with its label in seconds, without waiting for a file. The phone still works.

    Example: HHS emPOWER: ask how many Medicare users rely on electric medical equipment in a ZIP code and get the count back. Counts from 1 to 10 show as 11 to protect privacy.

    11 of our 57 sources are here today

Why this option

  • It works with what exists. Owners keep their data where it is. Nothing new to buy or install.
  • Every step pays off on its own. Nothing waits on a statewide agreement.
  • It's honest about gaps. A missing fact shows up as a phone call, with who to call and what to ask.
  • It has worked elsewhere. Transit agencies grew a shared format step by step, and HHS emPOWER already shares health counts without sharing records.
Show the evidence (3)
  • The State already asks for labels: CDO-003 Data Catalog Guidelines (28 August 2025) say each agency should keep a catalog and metadata for its data assets and name a data steward for each. Both State portals already publish DCAT-US records with owner, contact, address and last-changed date on every dataset. source, checked 2026-11-14
  • Ladders and thin standards have worked elsewhere: the 5-star open data scheme lets a publisher stop at any step; GTFS "started with a collaboration between TriMet in Portland, Oregon, and Google" and now reports "over 10,000 agencies in 100+ countries". source, checked 2026-11-14
  • A level-3 source for people already exists and protects privacy: HHS emPOWER answers with counts per ZIP and masks 1 to 10 as 11. source, checked 2026-11-14

What this looks like for a planner: one printable brief per county and hazard. Every number names its label. Every gap says who to ask. The next step shows it working on real public data.
