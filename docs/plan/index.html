Label & Ladder: an AI-developed plan for ESF-6 AFN planning in Hawaiʻi

HAIC 2026 · AI-Developed Planning and Improvement Challenge · DHS: “Enhancing ESF-6 Planning Through AI-Supported Insights”. Data as of 2026-11-14. Every number below is produced by the build in this repository (uv run build.py); the live versions are on the showcase page.

Planning aid. Supports, does not replace, county emergency plans. Not an official shelter list.

1. In one page

DHS named the root issue itself: “The information exists. It just isn’t connected.” (DHS pitch, slide 5). Information about people with access and functional needs (AFN), hazard exposure and partner resources sits with many owners, in different shapes, with no shared words and no date saying when anyone last checked it.

We considered three roads (section 3) and chose the third: standardize the label, not the system.

  • Label: every data source gets the same one-page description: owner, what it covers, area level, unit, as of, last confirmed, how to get it, privacy level, limits, and who to ask.
  • Ladder: each sector climbs at its own pace from a phone call (level 0) to a schedule (1), a file at a stable address (2), and a service that answers with counts per area (3). Each rung is useful even if the next never comes.

What works today, from public data only:

  • 432 of 432 census tracts with residents, mapped for hurricane, wildfire, tsunami and flood (DHS goal 1).
  • Needs estimates for every county and hazard across the CMIST areas, with low / likely / high ranges, checked against past Hawaiʻi events (DHS goal 2).
  • 16 printable briefs, one per county and hazard, rebuilt from the sources in under a second. Of 560 brief questions, 188 are answered from a labeled source, 240 are answered with a stated limit, and 132 still need a phone call, each naming who to ask and what to ask (DHS goal 3).
  • 57 labeled sources: 11 at level 0, 19 at level 1, 16 at level 2, 11 at level 3.

Our ask: one county and one timed tabletop to measure goal 3, and DHS’s answers to the questions in section 10.

2. The root issue, with evidence

The brief describes the symptoms (pp. 3–4). Our research found six causes behind them. Each is backed by a source we opened and saved (data/research/).

Cause DHS’s words What we found
No shared words “each County maintains its own definitions of sheltering options” (p. 3) FEMA’s ESF#6 Shelter System spells the four counties 9 ways across Hawaiʻi’s 729 records. Kauaʻi calls its sites “refuge areas … an evacuation option of last resort”; Honolulu uses four site types.
No date anyone checked “Data quality varies in completeness and format” (p. 3) Neither State data catalog fills the fields that say how current a dataset is. The State’s only open shelter list says “Date Updated: June 18, 2012”.
Rebuilt every event, never kept “Recompiling shelter and refuge lists … for each planning cycle and each event” (p. 4) HI-EMA posts shelters “when they are opened”. Maui County publishes no standing list. Few past events publish a shelter count at all.
Shared systems with empty boxes “making sure shelters have power generation, transportation, language support …” (p. 4) Of FEMA’s 729 Hawaiʻi records, 554 say nothing about a generator and 578 nothing about wheelchair access.
Records or nothing “Privacy rules limit sharing of client-level system data” (p. 3) Utilities keep medical-needs customer lists but publish no counts by area. HHS emPOWER shows the alternative: counts per ZIP, never records.
Nobody holds the whole picture “Partner resources and roles are not consistently mapped” (p. 3) No state agency publishes a current statewide shelter or partner-resource roll-up.

3. Three roads, and why we chose the third

Road What it gets right Verdict
Build another map or dashboard Answers the brief’s “no single, current view” (p. 3). We draw a map too. Not enough on its own: a map is only as current as what feeds it, and copies today’s stale and blank fields into a nicer picture.
One shared system, or a strict standard every sector must adopt One place to look. Rejected as the first step: for shelters that system already exists (FEMA ESF#6, synced daily with the Red Cross) and its key boxes are empty. A mandate also waits on the slowest sector.
Label what exists, then climb a ladder Starts today with nothing new; each rung pays off alone; AI agents can work at every rung. Chosen. Its weakness is that it needs owners to keep labels current; stage 2 below makes that a seasonal habit with a named owner.

Further decisions, each in data/argument.json with its evidence:

  • Counts per area, never people. The brief rules out individual registries and tracking (p. 6).
  • Reuse existing standards’ field names (the State catalogs already publish DCAT-US); add only area level, last confirmed, privacy level and ladder level.
  • Printable briefs and a static site, because the plan must work in “high-stress, low-connectivity conditions” (p. 5). The site calls no outside service, so it runs from a laptop with Wi-Fi off.
  • Numbers from plain code; AI to build, research, check and draft (section 8).
  • Four hazards, as the brief’s scope and goals name; earthquake (mentioned once, p. 4) is a later addition on the same ladder.

4. What the plan does today: Map, Model, Match

Map. For every census tract: the share of residents inside each hazard zone (2020 Census blocks) and AFN indicators by CMIST area from the Census ACS 2020–2024 with 90% margins of error:

CMIST Indicators (tract level unless noted)
Communication limited-English households; hearing and vision difficulty; detailed languages (county level, ACS microdata)
Medical electricity-dependent Medicare beneficiaries (HHS emPOWER, ZIP and county only; never split into tracts)
Independence difficulty walking or climbing stairs, bathing or dressing, doing errands alone
Safety and support kūpuna 65+ living alone; children under 5; difficulty remembering or deciding
Transportation households without a vehicle

Hazard zones: State tsunami evacuation zone; FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area; wildfire = within 500 m of land rated high or very high Wildfire Hazard Potential (USFS); hurricane = the whole island. Unsheltered Point-in-Time counts and average daily visitors are kept as separate county lines. Shelter sites come from each county’s own lists and event announcements since 2018, each with its source, and are flagged by zone.

Model. People likely to need a public shelter = people in the zone × a published shelter-seeking rate (low / likely / high; tsunami and flood rates are labeled team assumptions because no published rate exists). Needs follow from per-person factors in data/factors.csv (51 factors: 40 published with page and quote, 11 team assumptions). CMIST groups overlap and are never added; people and households are never added.

Match. Each need goes to a proposed lead and support partners (from the SESF #6 Annex, the State Emergency Operations Plan and the DHS brief, or marked “team proposal”), with confirmed capacity left blank and the gap written as “unknown: confirm with the owner”. Decision points per hazard are tied to official alerts (CPHC, NWS, PTWC). Resident messages are drafted for before, during and after, with formats and channels; translations are marked AI drafts that need native-speaker review, and Chuukese and Marshallese are routed to human translators.

5. DHS’s three measurable goals

Goal 1: hazard-specific AFN maps, every county, 100% of census tracts. Met: 432 of 432 tracts with residents for all four hazards (the 29 other tracts in the Census list are water or have no residents, and are listed). Six of the 432 had 202 residents in the 2020 Census but an ACS estimate of 0, so their estimates show 0. Medical need has no public tract source, so it is shown at ZIP and county level and says so.

Goal 2: needs estimates validated against past events. The same formula runs on the area each past event affected, without tuning. Proposed margin (for DHS to agree): the published count falls inside the model’s range.

Event County Published count Model likely (range) Result
Maui wildfires, 2023-08-08 Maui 2,100 800 (600–2,190) inside the range
Central Maui brush fire, 2019-07-11 Maui 479 120 (89–330) published 4.0× higher
Tsunami warning, 2025-07-29 Hawaiʻi 1,500 660 (260–1,590) inside the range
Tsunami warning, 2025-07-29 Kauaʻi 1,800 560 (220–1,340) published 3.2× higher
Kona low storm, 2026-03-20 Honolulu 131 240 (120–480) inside the range
Hurricane Lane, 2018-08-23 Honolulu 1,114 190,220 (60,070–370,420) model 171× higher
Hurricane Lala, 2026-08-16 Honolulu 247 190,220 (60,070–370,420) model 770× higher
Hurricane Lowell, 2026-09-08 Kauaʻi 301 14,010 (4,420–27,280) model 47× higher

The full table (13 main rows, plus secondary rows) is on the showcase page. Honest reading: wildfire and tsunami turnouts land in the range or above it, so the high end is the safer planning figure. The hurricane rate is the Hawaiʻi Hurricane Behavioral Study’s planning rate for a direct hit; every hurricane in the main rows was a near miss, so the model is far above those nights. The one direct hit on record, ʻIniki on Kauaʻi in 1992, had about 7,000 people in shelters two days later, inside the model’s range (a secondary row: the source is a newspaper count). Use it to size what a direct hit would take, not to staff a near miss. Few events publish a count at all, which is itself one of the gaps this plan names.

Goal 3: time to a coordinated needs-and-resources plan. The brief lists the manual steps: “Pulling program and provider reports, cross-referencing lists with hazard zones, contacting partners … to confirm shelter, transport, staffing, and supply capacity” (p. 3). The briefs do the pulling and cross-referencing in advance; calling shrinks to the lines still marked unknown, each with who to ask.

  • Baseline (team assumption, to confirm with DHS): about 3 working days of staff time for one county and hazard.
  • Target: under 1 working day: the brief prints in minutes and the rest is the remaining calls.
  • Measured so far: machine time only (all 16 briefs rebuild in under a second). The baseline and target are not yet measured with planners.
  • Measurement plan: a timed tabletop with one county: the same planners, the same scenario, once the current way and once with the brief. Report both times and the number of calls each needed.

6. The ladder, and climbing one rung

Level Name What the sector provides What an AI agent can do
0 Phone call The information exists; you ask and wait Name the owner and the question; draft the request
1 Spreadsheet on a schedule Published or sent on a known schedule Read it, quote it with its dates, warn when it is past due
2 File at a stable address A program can fetch it and join it by place Fetch and combine it with other sources on its own
3 Service that answers questions A program can ask it by place; about people, it answers with counts only, never records Ask it directly during planning or an event

The showcase includes a practice demonstration (labeled “Practice data” everywhere): Maui County keeps one dated sheet of its shelter sites. No quantity is invented; a capacity appears only where a public record already lists one. Even this one sheet moves the site-list line from “with a limit” to “answered” in all four Maui briefs, and the sites-in-zone line in three of them; the eight partner-resource lines stay phone calls until partners post their own sheets. That is the migration in miniature.

Each stage is worth doing even if the next never comes. All owners are proposals.

8. Privacy, guardrails and how AI was used

Guardrails. No record about any person, no registry, no tracking (brief p. 6). Counts per area only, with small counts hidden by their owner. County plans and alerting stay in charge; nothing here is deployed into DHS systems. Listed is not open: a site is never called open, safe or available unless a current official source says so.

How AI was used. AI agents built the data pipeline and the pages from a written design contract; researched sources, saving each page with its address, fetch time and checksum; ran a second, independent agent over each research note to re-open every citation and correct what did not match; and drafted the labels, messages and plan text. AI produced no number by itself: every count, share, estimate and back-test row comes from plain code that anyone can re-run, and the model was not tuned to past events.

What a person still has to review (not yet done): every role, decision point and trigger, by the county and State ESF-6; message drafts and translations, by native speakers and trusted messengers; the factors marked team assumption; the shelter site lists and their zone flags. A second, independent AI review of the model, factors, labels and plan text (2026-11-15) re-derived the headline numbers from the inputs and re-checked every published factor quote against its saved source; it found no error in a headline number, and the wording, citation and back-test-summary errors it did find are fixed. A person still has to review the items above.

9. Limits

  • Tract AFN counts in a zone assume AFN residents are spread like everyone else in the tract.
  • The wildfire rule (within 500 m of high hazard land) is broad on Oʻahu (484,000 people); the narrower mapped-hazard rule is shown beside it in each brief. Counties should choose.
  • Hurricane totals assume the whole island is affected: an upper planning bound, with the largest single community also reported.
  • Detailed languages for Maui and Kauaʻi are published only for the two counties combined.
  • emPOWER counts only Medicare beneficiaries; counts from 1 to 10 are hidden by HHS.
  • No official source gives a current capacity for any county’s sites; FEMA records list capacities without dates.

10. Questions for DHS

  1. How long does it take today to put together a needs-and-resources plan for one county and one hazard, and would one county join a timed tabletop to measure it before and after?
  2. Would each county share one shelter sheet per season (site type, planned capacity, access, power, pets, last confirmed), published or shared with ESF-6 partners only?
  3. Who should own the shared words: HI-EMA and DHS as an attachment to the SESF #6 Annex, the Chief Data Officer as a State data standard, or both?
  4. For goal 2, is “the published shelter count falls inside the model’s range” an acceptable margin, and which past events should count?
  5. Which partners who hold people-level records would share counts per area, and under what agreement?
